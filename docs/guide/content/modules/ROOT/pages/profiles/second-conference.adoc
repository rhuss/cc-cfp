When your next proposal goes to a new edition of KubeCon + CloudNativeCon, the profile you already have stays in place.
The new edition gets an event on that profile, with its own dates, tracks, location, and session type variations.
When cfp refreshes a profile for a new edition, it asks before it touches anything in the family profile.
The rubric, the rules, the rejection reasons, and the bio settings come from the profile, whichever edition you draft for.

A second conference is different.
It gets a profile of its own, and almost every part of that profile can look different from KubeCon's.
You build it with `/cfp:profile new`, and xref:profiles/new.adoc[Research a new conference] walks you through that.
This page picks up where that one stops.
It goes through the differences you're likely to meet and says, for each one, what cfp does about it.

Let's use ExampleConf for that, a fictional conference made up for this page.
Its values aren't taken from any real call for proposals.
The excerpts below come from its profile file, `profiles/exampleconf/profile.md` in your store, and every key in them is one the profile schema knows.
If a term such as event, verification label, or relevance field is new to you, xref:profiles/index.adoc[How profiles work] explains it, and xref:profiles/kubecon.adoc[The shipped KubeCon profile] shows the KubeCon values to compare with.

== Another submission platform

The shipped KubeCon profile says `platform: sessionize`.
ExampleConf runs its own submission site:

[source,yaml]
----
name: ExampleConf
platform: exampleconf-portal
----

`platform` is free text.
The research checklist gives `sessionize`, `pretalx`, and `cfp.dev` as examples, and the profile validation doesn't check the value at all.
`/cfp:profile show` prints it in its header.

Whatever the platform, nothing is submitted on your behalf: you paste the texts into the conference site yourself.
When the site's form sits behind a login, screenshots of it are the most reliable source for the profile, and xref:profiles/new.adoc[Research a new conference] shows how cfp takes them in.

== Session types of its own

ExampleConf has no panels or tutorials.
It has short talks, workshops with at least two instructors, and ten-minute demo slots:

[source,yaml]
----
session_types:
  - id: talk
    name: Talk
    duration_min: 25
    min_speakers: 1
    max_speakers: 2
    notes: ""
  - id: workshop
    name: Hands-on Workshop
    duration_min: 90
    min_speakers: 2
    max_speakers: 3
    notes: At least two instructors.
  - id: demo
    name: Demo Slot
    duration_min: 10
    min_speakers: 1
    max_speakers: 1
    notes: ""
----

`/cfp:draft` lists these session types with their name, duration, and speaker range, and asks you to pick one.
When the type you pick has `min_speakers` above 1, like the workshop here, cfp tells you that a solo speaker can't satisfy it and asks whether you have co-speakers.
When the form has a `format` field, its default is the name of the session type you picked.

An event can still change a session type for one edition, or remove it, through `session_type_overrides`.
The session type ids also matter for rules, as you'll see in <<rules>>.

== A rubric with its own criteria and thresholds

KubeCon scores five criteria on a scale up to 5.
ExampleConf publishes three criteria on a scale up to 4, and no pass threshold:

[source,yaml]
----
rubric:
  scale_max: 4
  pass_average: 3.0
  pass_min_criterion: 2.0
  criteria:
    - id: clarity
      name: Clarity
      description: 1 is hard to follow. 4 is a clear problem, a clear plan for the session, and takeaways in full sentences.
    - id: usefulness
      name: Usefulness
      description: 1 gives attendees nothing to try. 4 leaves them with something they can use the next working day.
    - id: fit
      name: Fit
      description: 1 is off topic for ExampleConf. 4 is a topic the ExampleConf audience keeps asking for.
----

The committee review scores exactly these criteria.
The reviewer takes the criterion ids from what it's given for the proposal, never from its own instructions, and cfp rejects a review that misses a score or a comment for any of them.
Each score lies between 0 and `scale_max`, in half steps at most.
The reviewer reads each `description` literally, as the meaning of each level, so the research puts the conference's wording for the levels into it.

`cfp` computes the verdict itself.
A proposal passes when the average is at least `pass_average` and no criterion is below `pass_min_criterion`.
When the rubric leaves out either key, the review uses 4.0 for `pass_average` and 3.0 for `pass_min_criterion`.

ExampleConf publishes no numbers, so where do 3.0 and 2.0 come from?
For a conference like that, the research sets a defensible default and records it as an assumption in the profile's drafting guidance (see <<guidance>>).
A rubric without any criteria stops the committee review from running, and the validation reports an approved profile whose rubric has none.

One more difference concerns recordings.
When none of the proposal's speaker resources is a recording, the reviewer caps the Speaker criterion at the level the rubric gives for "no recorded examples", which is 4 on the KubeCon scale (lower when the bio shows no depth in the topic either).
A rubric that names no such level has no cap.
ExampleConf's rubric has no Speaker criterion and names no such level, so there's no cap.
xref:user/review.adoc[Committee review and voice pass] explains how the scoring and the cap work.

== Rules that are mostly custom

The shipped KubeCon profile has seven rules, and none of them is of kind `custom`.
ExampleConf has one rule that cfp can compute and three that it can't:

[source,yaml]
----
rules:
  - id: speaker-count
    kind: speaker_count
    severity: disqualify
    params: {}
    source: https://exampleconf.example/2027/cfp/
    text: Each session type has a fixed number of speakers.
  - id: complete-at-close
    kind: custom
    severity: disqualify
    params: {}
    source: https://exampleconf.example/2027/cfp/
    text: Proposals with an empty required field at the CFP close are not reviewed.
  - id: workshop-in-person
    kind: custom
    severity: disqualify
    params:
      session_type: workshop
    source: https://exampleconf.example/2027/cfp/
    text: Every workshop instructor attends in person.
  - id: workshop-install-list
    kind: custom
    severity: warn
    params:
      session_type: workshop
    source: https://exampleconf.example/2027/cfp/
    text: A workshop abstract names the software attendees install before the session.
----

The pre-flight goes through the rules the profile lists.
It computes six kinds from your store: submission limit, repeat talks, track, speaker count, and the two panel rules.
For `speaker-count`, it checks whether you plus your co-speakers fit the chosen session type's minimum and maximum.
A computed rule that ExampleConf doesn't list, such as a submission limit, isn't checked.

A `custom` rule has no evaluator of its own.
The pre-flight hands each one, with its id, severity, parameters, text, and source, to Claude, which judges it against your proposal from the rule's text.
Here's how the three ExampleConf rules come out:

* `params.session_type` narrows a rule to one session type.
For a talk or a demo proposal, neither workshop rule applies.
* `complete-at-close` is a rule about incomplete proposals, so it's violated as long as a required field is still empty.
* For a workshop proposal, `workshop-in-person` depends on something your store doesn't hold.
Because the rule can disqualify, cfp quotes the rule and asks you whether your proposal meets it.
* For a workshop proposal, `workshop-install-list` is judged from the drafted text as met or violated, and as a `warn` rule it doesn't block `ready`.

A violated custom rule takes its severity from the profile.
So a violated `disqualify` rule blocks `ready` like a computed rule, and a `warn` rule doesn't.
A rule without a `severity` counts as `disqualify`.
xref:user/preflight.adoc[Pre-flight, counts, and the paste view] lists the four verdicts and what each of your answers leads to.
How to write a custom rule's text so that it can be checked is part of xref:profiles/new.adoc[Research a new conference].

== Audience levels worded differently

KubeCon offers `Any`, `Beginner`, `Intermediate`, and `Advanced`.
ExampleConf asks a different question, and its help text defines each answer:

[source,yaml]
----
fields:
  # title and abstract come first
  - id: audience_level
    label: Who is this for?
    required: true
    kind: choice
    limit:
    limit_status: unverified
    source: ""
    options:
      - New to the topic
      - Uses it at work
      - Builds or maintains it
    audience: public
    guidance: "New to the topic: no prior experience needed. Uses it at work: attendees run it in production. Builds or maintains it: attendees contribute to it or extend it."
----

`/cfp:draft` asks you to pick one of the field's options and never generates the answer.
`cfp` accepts only one of the options, matched without regard to case, and stores it as the form spells it.
Saving the field also updates the level that the pre-flight reads.

The pre-flight's audience level check compares your abstract with the offered levels in form order.
When the field's `guidance` defines the levels, as ExampleConf's does, the check uses those definitions.
On a mismatch it names the better level exactly as the form words it, for example `Builds or maintains it`.
The committee reviewer checks the level against the content as well.

So keep the field id `audience_level`, whatever the form calls the field.
The research uses shared ids like this one so that the draft flow recognizes the same field across conferences.
When the profile has no `audience_level` field, the pre-flight skips the comparison.

== A form without a speaker bio

ExampleConf's form asks for no speaker bio with a proposal:

[source,yaml]
----
bio_on_form: false
bio_limit:
bio_limit_status: unverified
----

Only an explicit `bio_on_form: false` switches the bio off.
The research sets it only when screenshots cover the complete form and show no bio.

With the bio off, `/cfp:draft` says in one line that the form has no speaker bio and attaches none.
The paste view prints no bio section.
The review packet carries an empty bio, and the committee reviewer never counts that against the proposal.
A bio you stored on the proposal anyway is still counted, with no limit and the status `not-on-form`, as xref:user/preflight.adoc[Pre-flight, counts, and the paste view] shows.

== Another reviewer-only relevance field

KubeCon's relevance field is `value_to_community`.
ExampleConf has three text fields that only reviewers read:

[source,yaml]
----
fields:
  # title, abstract, and audience_level come first
  - id: previously_presented
    label: Has this talk been given before?
    required: false
    kind: longtext
    limit: 300
    limit_status: unverified
    source: ""
    audience: reviewer
    guidance: Where and when, and what changed since.
  - id: program_notes
    label: Notes for the program team
    required: true
    kind: longtext
    limit: 800
    limit_status: unverified
    source: ""
    audience: reviewer
    guidance: Why this talk belongs at ExampleConf this year.
  - id: room_needs
    label: Room and equipment needs
    required: false
    kind: longtext
    limit: 200
    limit_status: unverified
    source: ""
    audience: reviewer
    guidance: Power, network, and seating the session needs. Workshops list the laptop setup attendees bring.
----

`cfp` picks exactly one relevance field.
ExampleConf has no `benefits` field, so cfp takes the first reviewer-only long-text field in form order, leaving out `previously_presented`.
That's `program_notes`, and `/cfp:profile show` reports it as the `relevance_field`.

`/cfp:draft` writes `program_notes` with its guidance for relevance fields: who benefits by persona, why now, what's new, credentials and evidence, and the data shown, without restating the abstract.
That guidance says other conferences have such a field under other names and treats them all the same way.
`room_needs` is a reviewer-only text field too, but cfp drafts it from its own `guidance`, like any other text field.

The committee reviewer checks that the relevance field adds what the abstract doesn't have.
When it restates the abstract, the reviewer lowers the relevance criterion, or the closest criterion when the rubric has none by that name.
ExampleConf's rubric has no criterion named relevance.

[#guidance]
== Drafting guidance of its own

Every field carries its own `guidance`, and `/cfp:draft` uses it when it drafts that field.
ExampleConf's `room_needs` above is one example.

On top of that, each profile has a `## Drafting guidance` section in the body of its file, below the front matter.
`/cfp:profile new` fills it with what reviewers reward and punish, its pass assumption when the conference published no number, and any historic limits with the URL they came from.
For ExampleConf it could start like this:

[source,markdown]
----
## Drafting guidance

ExampleConf publishes its three criteria but no pass threshold.
Assumption: pass_average 3.0 and pass_min_criterion 2.0 on the 4-point scale.
The program team prefers a live demo over slides only.
----

`cfp` loads this section from the profile file.
`/cfp:profile show` prints it in full, and the committee review packet carries it too.

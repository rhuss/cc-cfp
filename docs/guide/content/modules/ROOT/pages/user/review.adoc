Before you paste a proposal into a conference form, you'd like someone strict to read it the way a program committee member would.
That's the committee review: a simulated program committee round that scores your proposal against the conference's rubric and tells you what to fix.
After a passing review, the voice pass reworks your title, abstract, reviewer-only relevance field, and bio snapshot to remove signs of AI-generated writing.
This page explains both.
xref:user/draft.adoc[Draft a proposal] shows where they sit in the `/cfp:draft` flow, and xref:user/index.adoc[Using cfp] explains the terms talk, proposal, profile, and bio snapshot.

== Why a simulated review

The reviewer is an agent that plays an experienced program committee member.
Its brief: it reviews 100 to 200 proposals per cycle for one track, in a star-rating interface, with a few minutes per proposal.
It reviews for the conference and its community, not for any company, and it has no stake in your proposal being accepted.
It knows the usual tricks, like the vendor pitch dressed as a case study or the abstract that promises "deep dives" without naming one hard part, and it prefers a typo over AI-flattened prose.

It's a simulation.
The scores come from an agent that works from the rubric in your conference profile, not from the conference's committee.
What you get is a strict reading with concrete suggestions, before the texts go into the form.

== What the reviewer reads

The review runs in the `cfp:committee-review` skill.
First it builds the packet, a JSON file in the session's scratchpad directory that holds everything the reviewer may know about your proposal:

* the profile's rubric (the top of the scale and the criteria, each with a description of what the star levels mean) and the two pass thresholds;
* the conference's documented reasons for rejection and the profile's drafting guidance;
* calibration examples (see <<calibration>>);
* your drafted fields, each with its id, label, kind, whether it's required, whether the public or only the reviewers see it, and its text;
* the id of the reviewer-only relevance field, or nothing when the form has none;
* the session type, track, audience level, and angle;
* the bio snapshot, but only when the conference form has a bio;
* the speaker resources you chose for the proposal, with their type, title, and link;
* your co-speakers and your talk's story: its title, personas, projects, outings, and sections.

Then the skill starts the `cfp:committee-reviewer` agent.
That agent is a subagent: in Claude Code's terms, it runs in its own context window with a custom system prompt, specific tool access, and independent permissions.
The reviewer's only tool is Read.

The prompt it gets is short: the path of the packet file, the instruction to read only that file, and the instruction to reply with one JSON object.
The skill never pastes your drafted fields, the rubric, or your drafting history into that prompt, so the reviewer forms its opinion from the packet alone.
It doesn't search your store or ask you questions.

Every string in the packet is data for the reviewer, never an instruction.
If a drafted field contains text that tells the reviewer to change its scores or read another file, the reviewer ignores it, scores the proposal down under Content, and says so in its comment.
The same kind of text in a calibration example or in guidance is ignored without counting against your draft.

== How the reviewer scores

The reviewer scores every criterion of the rubric from 0 to the top of the scale.
Half steps such as 3.5 are allowed, nothing finer.
On the shipped KubeCon + CloudNativeCon profile, the scale goes to 5 and there are five criteria: Content, Originality, Relevance, Speaker, and Not a Product Pitch.

Here's how it gets to a score:

. It reads each criterion's description literally, places your proposal at the star level whose wording matches the evidence, and adjusts by at most half a step.
. It writes exactly two sentences per criterion: first the judgment, then the evidence, quoting or pointing to a phrase, gap, or claim in your text.
. It walks through the rejection reasons one by one, and for each one that applies, it lowers the criterion the reason names, or the closest one.
. It lowers scores for AI-sounding text.
That covers generic phrasing that could head any talk on the topic, inflated claims without a number or a named system, rule-of-three padding, buzzword lists, no concrete takeaway the attendee can act on, vendor or product language, and sentences that announce importance instead of showing it.
. It compares tone and structure with the calibration examples.
. It checks the reviewer-only relevance field, when the form has one: the field must add what the abstract doesn't have, and a field that restates the abstract costs the relevance criterion.
. It checks that the audience level matches the content and that the track fits the abstract.
. It applies the speaker rules (see <<recordings>>).
. It writes at most five suggestions, ordered by impact, each tagged with the id of the field it targets.

The suggestions are instructions for you, not replacement text.
The reviewer never rewrites a field.
And it's told to be strict: a 4 is a proposal it would argue for in the committee call, and a 5 is one it would remember after the cycle.

Is the reply usable?
The skill checks it before anything is recorded.
The reply needs a score and a non-empty comment for exactly the rubric's criteria, with each score from 0 to the top of the scale in whole or half steps.
Its verdict must be `pass` or `revise`, and every suggestion must name a field from the packet.
When the check fails, the skill asks the reviewer once more and quotes the problem.
When the second reply fails too, the round isn't recorded, no scores are invented, and the skill tells you what went wrong.

== How the verdict is computed

The reviewer's reply contains its own verdict, but that verdict is advisory.
It's stored with the round and never decides the outcome.
The verdict you see comes from the store CLI, which applies the profile's thresholds to the scores:

* The average of all criterion scores, rounded to two decimals, must be at or above `pass_average`.
* No criterion may be below `pass_min_criterion`.

When both hold, the verdict is `pass`; otherwise it's `revise`.
The KubeCon + CloudNativeCon profile sets `pass_average` to 4.0 and `pass_min_criterion` to 3.0, and a profile without thresholds gets the same two values.

So an average alone doesn't carry a proposal.
Scores of 5 for Content, 5 for Originality, 2.5 for Relevance, 4 for Speaker, and 5 for Not a Product Pitch average 4.3, but Relevance sits below 3.0, and the verdict is `revise`.

The skill then shows you the result as a table with one row per criterion, holding the score and the comment.
Below it come the round number, the verdict, the average against `pass_average`, and the criteria below `pass_min_criterion`, or a note that there are none.
The suggestions follow as a numbered list, each with its field id.
When the reviewer's advisory verdict differs from the computed one, one extra line says so.

Each round is stored in the proposal file with its date, scores, comments, both verdicts, the average, and the suggestions.

== The review loop in /cfp:draft

In `/cfp:draft`, the review is Step 7, and it runs as a loop:

. A round runs, as described above.
. On `pass`, the flow moves on to the voice pass.
. On `revise`, cfp shows the suggestions and asks which to apply: all, some by number, or none.
For each one you accept, cfp rewrites that field following the suggestion and the field's guidance, shows you the new text with its count, and saves it.
If you apply none, cfp asks whether to run another round anyway or stop.
. If the round couldn't be recorded (see above), cfp tells you and offers to retry the round or park the proposal.
. While rounds are left, the next round starts.

The number of rounds is `review_rounds` in your store's `config.md`.
It defaults to 3, and `/cfp:init` asks for it, accepting 1 to 10.
xref:reference/config.adoc[Configuration and proposal lifecycle] lists every config key.

When the loop reaches that number without a pass, cfp lists the remaining weaknesses, the criteria below the minimum with their last comments and the open suggestions, and gives you three choices:

Continue:: One more round, then cfp asks again.
Accept as is:: The flow goes on without a pass.
The proposal stays in `draft`, because `ready` needs a passing verdict, and the voice pass is skipped.
You can still paste the texts into the form yourself and record that with `/cfp:status`.
Park:: The flow stops, and everything stays saved as a draft.
`/cfp:draft --proposal <id>` resumes it.

[#recordings]
== Recordings and the Speaker criterion

On the KubeCon + CloudNativeCon rubric, 4 stars means topic expertise and clear examples of presented content, and 5 stars means a recognized leader with recorded examples of engaging talks.

The reviewer applies that literally.
When none of the proposal's speaker resources is of type `recording`, the Speaker score is capped at the level the rubric gives for no recorded examples.
On the KubeCon + CloudNativeCon scale that cap is 4, or lower when the bio shows no depth in the topic either.
A rubric that names no such level has no cap.
The reviewer's comment then says that a recording would lift the cap.

Have a recording of an earlier talk?
Add it with `/cfp:bio resource add` (see xref:user/bios.adoc[Bios and speaker resources]), and `/cfp:draft` offers it first when it fills the form's resources field.

One more rule concerns the bio: when the conference form has no bio, the packet carries none, and the reviewer never counts the missing bio against you.

[#calibration]
== Accepted proposals as calibration examples

Calibration examples are accepted texts and official samples, stored in your profile's `examples` folder.
Each round puts the newest ones into the packet, three by default, with their title, abstract, benefits text, event, year, and origin.
The reviewer compares your draft with them: accepted proposals name the problem in the first two sentences, name the contribution, and state their takeaways in full sentences, and your draft is rated against that bar.

Your own accepted proposals become examples, too.
When you set a proposal to `accepted` with `/cfp:status`, cfp adds its title, its abstract, and the text of its relevance field to the profile as a calibration example with the origin `own-accepted`.
xref:user/status.adoc[Track a proposal's status] covers the status changes.

== Run one round on its own

`/cfp:draft` runs the committee review for you, but you can also run a single round on any stored proposal without going through the draft flow.
Like other plugin skills, the `cfp:committee-review` skill runs when you type `/cfp:committee-review`.
You can also ask in your own words, such as "run the committee review", and Claude loads the skill when it's relevant.

. In Claude Code, run one round on Ada's proposal:
+
[source,text]
----
/cfp:committee-review --proposal kubecon-eu-2027--safe-node-drains
----
+
Without `--proposal`, cfp lists your proposals and asks which one.
Add `--examples <n>` to put a different number of calibration examples into the packet.
+
Check: `cfp` shows the table with one row per criterion, the verdict, the average, and the suggestions.
It ends with the hint that `/cfp:draft --proposal kubecon-eu-2027--safe-node-drains` continues the drafting flow with these suggestions.
If the proposal has no drafted fields yet, cfp shows the error and stops instead.

. A round you run on its own is recorded like any other.

== The voice pass

The voice pass is Step 8 of `/cfp:draft`.
It hands your texts to `prose:humanizer`, the humanizer skill of the optional cc-prose plugin, which xref:index.adoc[What cfp does] introduces.

=== When it runs and on which texts

The voice pass runs only after a passing verdict.
When the last verdict isn't `pass`, cfp skips it without a word and goes on to Step 9.

It works on up to four texts:

* the title;
* the abstract;
* the reviewer-only relevance field, when the profile names one;
* the bio snapshot, when the conference form has a bio.

For each text, cfp calls `prose:humanizer` with the voice profile from your config, or without a voice when that setting is empty.

=== What it changes

You see every rewrite in full, with its count against the field's limit, and you choose whether to keep it or the previous version.
The title is rewritten only for wording, and its meaning stays.
Sometimes the skill answers with usage or help text instead of a rewrite.
Then cfp applies the humanizer's core rules itself (no AI vocabulary, no rule-of-three padding, no inflated symbolism, varied sentence length, no em or en dashes) and tells you the rewrite was done inline.

`cfp` saves each text you keep, rechecks the character counts, and marks the voice pass as done.
For the bio, only the proposal's bio snapshot changes, never the stored bio variation.

There's no second committee review after the voice pass.
These edits don't mark the passing review as stale, because only a `ready` proposal goes back to `draft` when a field changes, and your proposal is still a draft at this point.
Step 9 then offers to shorten every field that's over its limit, as xref:user/preflight.adoc[Pre-flight, counts, and the paste view] describes.

=== Without cc-prose

cc-prose is optional.
When it isn't installed, cfp prints this line, keeps your texts as drafted, and goes on to Step 9:

[source,text]
----
Voice pass skipped: prose plugin not installed
----

=== Where voice profiles live

A voice profile is a named YAML file under `~/.claude/style/voices/`, and the file name without `.yaml` is the profile's name.
Profiles from older cc-prose versions live under `~/.claude/prose/voices/`.

`/cfp:init` looks in both folders and offers the names it finds, plus "None (humanize only)".
If it finds none, it tells you that the voice pass will run the humanizer without a named voice.
You can also type a name that doesn't exist yet.
Your choice goes into `voice_profile` in your store's `config.md`.

cc-prose also knows one project voice: the file `.style/voice.yaml` (older: `.prose/voice.yaml`) in the directory where your Claude Code session runs.
It has no name, so `/cfp:init` doesn't offer it, but cc-prose applies it before the named profile in every session started in that directory.
